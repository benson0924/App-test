\chapter{Classical Information and Computation}
\label{ch:classical}

Before quantum mechanics enters the story, we need a precise language for \emph{classical} information: how bits encode data, how logic gates transform bits, how arithmetic is built from gates, and how classical computers are modeled mathematically. This chapter establishes the baseline against which quantum advantages are measured. Every concept here reappears---often in generalized form---when we study qubits, reversible circuits, and complexity classes such as \textbf{BQP}.

\section{States and Information}
\label{sec:states-info}

\subsection{Physical encoding}

Physical systems can encode information by occupying distinguishable states. A switch may be open or closed; a magnetic domain may point up or down. The abstraction that captures this binary distinction is the \emph{classical bit}: a variable $b\in\{0,1\}$.

For $n$ independent bits, a register holds one of $2^n$ distinct strings. If each bit is equally likely and independent, the number of configurations is
\begin{equation}
N = 2^n.
\label{eq:2n-states}
\end{equation}
For $n=3$, the eight strings are $000,001,010,011,100,101,110,111$. Bits may represent integers (via binary encoding), characters (via ASCII or Unicode), machine instructions, or any digital datum once a convention is fixed.

\subsection{Entropy and distinguishability}

In Shannon's theory, a uniform random $n$-bit string carries $n$ bits of entropy. Distinguishability is absolute: observing a bit yields a definite $0$ or $1$ with no residual uncertainty unless the physical medium is noisy---a theme that returns in Chapter~\ref{ch:qec} when we protect quantum information.

\begin{labbox}{1}{Binary State Explorer}
Navigate to \texttt{/playground/binary-states} on the companion website. Select register sizes $n=1,2,\ldots,8$ and verify that the state count matches~\eqref{eq:2n-states}. Toggle individual bits and observe how each flip changes the decimal interpretation of the string.
\end{labbox}

\begin{example}
How many distinct 10-bit registers exist? Using~\eqref{eq:2n-states} with $n=10$,
\[
N = 2^{10} = 1024.
\]
This is exactly one kibibit ($1\,\mathrm{KiB}$) of addressable classical states when each address is one byte-aligned block of ten bits.
\end{example}

\section{Binary Numbers}
\label{sec:binary}

\subsection{Positional notation}

Positional notation interprets a bit string $b_{n-1}b_{n-2}\cdots b_0$ with $b_j\in\{0,1\}$ as the integer
\begin{equation}
(b_{n-1}\cdots b_0)_2 = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} b_j\, 2^j.
\label{eq:binary-positional}
\end{equation}
The subscript~$2$ reminds us that digits are base-two. Leading zeros do not change value: $00101_2 = 101_2 = 5_{10}$.

\subsection{Conversion and arithmetic preview}

To convert from decimal to binary, repeated division by two extracts remainders as least-significant bits. Addition in binary uses the same positional rules as decimal, but carries propagate when a column sums to $2$ or more.

\begin{example}
Evaluate $1011_2$ in decimal:
\[
1011_2 = 1\cdot 2^3 + 0\cdot 2^2 + 1\cdot 2^1 + 1\cdot 2^0 = 8 + 0 + 2 + 1 = 11_{10}.
\]
\end{example}

\subsection{Fixed-width registers}

Real hardware uses fixed-width registers. An $n$-bit unsigned integer wraps on overflow modulo $2^n$. Two's-complement encoding represents signed integers; the most significant bit indicates sign. Quantum algorithms often treat bit strings as labels for computational basis states $\ket{b_{n-1}\cdots b_0}$, making binary conventions essential.

\section{Logic Gates}
\label{sec:logic-gates}

\subsection{Boolean variables and truth tables}

Boolean variables $A,B,C\in\{0,1\}$ combine through \emph{logic gates}. Table~\ref{tab:basic-gates} lists fundamental gates; each gate is a function $\{0,1\}^k\to\{0,1\}$ for some $k$.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Elementary logic gates and their outputs.}
\label{tab:basic-gates}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Gate & Symbol & Definition \\
\midrule
NOT & $\overline{A}$ & $\overline{0}=1$, $\overline{1}=0$ \\
AND & $A\land B$ & $1$ only if both inputs are $1$ \\
OR & $A\lor B$ & $1$ if at least one input is $1$ \\
XOR & $A\oplus B$ & $1$ if inputs differ \\
NAND & $\overline{A\land B}$ & negated AND \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

XOR is \emph{linear} over $\mathbb{F}_2$: $A\oplus B\oplus C$ is the parity of three bits. It is also its own inverse: $(A\oplus B)\oplus B = A$.

\end{labbox}

\subsection{Universal classical sets}

A gate set is \emph{universal} if any Boolean function can be built from it. $\{\mathrm{NAND}\}$ alone is universal: NOT is $\mathrm{NAND}(A,A)$, AND is $\overline{\mathrm{NAND}(A,B)}$, and OR follows from De Morgan. Reversible quantum gate sets play an analogous role in Chapter~\ref{ch:multi}.

\section{Boolean Algebra}
\label{sec:boolean}

\subsection{Axioms and identities}

Boolean algebra axiomatizes logic with operations $\land,\lor,\overline{\phantom{A}}$. Key identities include
\begin{align}
A \land 1 &= A, & A \lor 0 &= A, \\
A \land 0 &= 0, & A \lor 1 &= 1, \\
A \land \overline{A} &= 0, & A \lor \overline{A} &= 1.
\end{align}
Associativity, commutativity, and distributivity hold as in ordinary algebra, with the caveat that $A\land(B\lor C)=(A\land B)\lor(A\land C)$.

\subsection{De Morgan's laws}

De Morgan's laws relate negation and conjunction/disjunction:
\begin{equation}
\overline{A\land B} = \overline{A}\lor\overline{B},\qquad
\overline{A\lor B} = \overline{A}\land\overline{B}.
\label{eq:demorgan}
\end{equation}
These identities underpin NAND-based synthesis and appear in circuit optimization.

\begin{example}
Simplify $\overline{A\land(B\lor C)}$ using~\eqref{eq:demorgan}:
\begin{align*}
\overline{A\land(B\lor C)} &= \overline{A}\lor\overline{B\lor C} \\
&= \overline{A}\lor(\overline{B}\land\overline{C}).
\end{align*}
\end{example}

\subsection{Normal forms}

Any Boolean function admits a \emph{sum-of-products} (disjunctive normal form) representation as an OR of AND clauses. Minimization---finding the fewest gates---is NP-hard in general, foreshadowing complexity themes in Section~\ref{sec:complexity}.

\section{Adders}
\label{sec:adders}

\subsection{Half adder}

A \emph{half adder} adds two bits without incoming carry, producing sum $S$ and carry-out $C$:
\begin{equation}
S = A\oplus B,\qquad C = A\land B.
\label{eq:half-adder}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Full adder and ripple carry}

A \emph{full adder} includes carry-in $C_{\mathrm{in}}$:
\begin{align}
S &= A\oplus B\oplus C_{\mathrm{in}}, \label{eq:full-sum}\\
C_{\mathrm{out}} &= (A\land B)\lor\bigl(C_{\mathrm{in}}\land(A\oplus B)\bigr). \label{eq:full-carry}
\end{align}
Chaining $n$ full adders yields an $n$-bit ripple-carry adder. Worst-case delay is $O(n)$ gate layers because the carry must propagate from least to most significant bit---a \emph{depth} bottleneck that quantum adder designs also confront.

\begin{labbox}{3}{Binary Adder}
Use \texttt{/playground/binary-adder} to add multi-bit numbers and trace carries step by step. Compare ripple depth with your hand calculation of~\eqref{eq:full-sum}--\eqref{eq:full-carry}.
\end{labbox}

\begin{example}
Compute $1101_2 + 1011_2$:
\begin{align*}
  &\phantom{+}1101_2 \;(13_{10}) \\
  + &\phantom{+}1011_2 \;(11_{10}) \\
  \hline
  &11000_2 \;(24_{10}).
\end{align*}
Column-wise: $1+1=0$ carry $1$; $0+1+1=0$ carry $1$; $1+0+1=0$ carry $1$; $1+1+1=1$ carry $1$.
\end{example}

\section{Reversible Computation}
\label{sec:reversible}

\subsection{Information loss in irreversible gates}

A gate is \emph{reversible} if outputs uniquely determine inputs. NOT and XOR (with one output wired as copy) are reversible; AND is not---knowing output $0$ does not distinguish $(0,0)$ from $(0,1)$. Landauer's principle links irreversible erasure to minimum heat dissipation, motivating reversible architectures.

\subsection{Toffoli and Fredkin gates}

The \emph{Toffoli} (controlled-controlled-NOT) gate maps $(a,b,c)\mapsto(a,b,c\oplus ab)$. With $c=0$, it computes AND reversibly into the third wire. The \emph{Fredkin} (controlled-SWAP) gate conditionally exchanges two target bits. Any classical computation can be embedded in a reversible circuit using ancilla bits initialized to~$0$.

\begin{example}
To emulate $c = a\land b$ reversibly, start with $(a,b,0)$ and apply Toffoli to obtain $(a,b,a\land b)$. The original AND output is read from the third wire; $(a,b)$ remain available for later use.
\end{example}

Quantum evolution before measurement must be unitary (hence reversible). Classical reversible logic is the direct template for quantum circuit synthesis.

\section{Classical Error Correction}
\label{sec:classical-ecc}

\subsection{Repetition code}

Noise flips bits with small probability $p$. The three-bit \emph{repetition code} encodes logical $0$ as $0_L=000$ and logical $1$ as $1_L=111$. A single bit flip yields a word at Hamming distance~$1$ from one codeword and distance~$2$ from the other; \emph{majority vote} decoding corrects one error.

\subsection{Parity checks}

Parity $p=a\oplus b$ summarizes two bits in one check bit. Longer block codes add multiple parity checks to locate errors via \emph{syndromes}. The quantum three-qubit bit-flip code (Chapter~\ref{ch:qec}) is a direct quantum analogue.

\begin{example}
Codeword $101$ suffers a flip on the middle bit, becoming $111$. Majority vote: two ones $\Rightarrow$ decode as $1$. The syndrome ``even number of ones in first two bits'' would flag an error in more elaborate schemes.
\end{example}

\section{Computational Complexity}
\label{sec:complexity}

\subsection{Asymptotic growth}

Algorithm cost is measured as a function of input size $n$. Common classes include $O(1)$, $O(\log n)$, $O(n)$, $O(n\log n)$, $O(n^2)$, and $O(2^n)$. Exponential growth renders problems intractable for large $n$ on any physically realizable classical machine.

\subsection{P, NP, and BQP preview}

\textbf{P} is the class of decision problems solvable in polynomial time on a deterministic Turing machine. \textbf{NP} contains problems whose \emph{yes} certificates are verifiable in polynomial time; NP-complete problems are the hardest in NP. Quantum computers do \emph{not} automatically solve NP-complete problems in polynomial time.

\textbf{BQP} (bounded-error quantum polynomial time) is the quantum analogue of P: problems solvable by a quantum computer with error probability $<1/3$ in polynomial time. Factoring (Shor) and unstructured search (Grover) illustrate problems where quantum algorithms offer provable advantages over known classical methods for specific tasks.

\section{Turing Machines}
\label{sec:turing}

\subsection{The model}

A \emph{Turing machine} consists of an infinite tape, a head that reads/writes symbols, a finite state controller, and a transition function. At each step the head reads a symbol, writes a symbol, moves left or right, and updates state. Despite its simplicity, the model captures what is \emph{classically computable}.

\subsection{Church--Turing thesis}

The Church--Turing thesis asserts that any physically realizable \emph{classical} computation can be simulated by a Turing machine with at most polynomial overhead. Quantum computation extends the physical model: the open question is whether all quantum processes admit efficient classical simulation. Evidence from Shor's algorithm suggests the answer is no for some problems.

\begin{example}
A Turing machine adding two $n$-bit binary numbers can scan the tape once per bit with carry, using $O(n)$ steps and $O(n)$ tape cells---polynomial in the input size, hence membership in P for this functional problem.
\end{example}
